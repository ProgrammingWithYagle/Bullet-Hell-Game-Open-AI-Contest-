# The Cart at the Gate

Original working scene for Grave Accord's opening volume. This is an authoring draft, not a playable replacement or a completed chapter. Senn Arlow and Renn Tallow are proposed recurring side characters from the cast plan. The confrontation precedes the Hollow Constable fight; the larger bell mystery remains unresolved.

The cart stopped with its front wheel in the gate's drainage groove.

For a moment Senn Arlow kept pulling. Their boots scraped on the stones. The two people seated beneath the cart's canvas cover leaned forward with them, as if the extra effort might persuade the wheel.

It did not.

“Don't pull harder,” Tamsin said through the bars.

Senn looked up. “I had reached that conclusion.”

“The brake's caught under the rim.”

“I know where it is. I can't reach it while I hold this.”

The gate shuddered. Senn put a shoulder against the nearer leaf, keeping a gap no wider than their arm. On the far side of the passage, a wheel turned through a quarter circle and stopped. Something behind it dragged metal over stone.

Mara set down her rifle before she approached the bars.

“How many with you?”

“Two on the cart. Two who can walk. One who says he can.”

“I can,” said the man nearest the opening.

“Then you can help me by sitting down,” Senn replied.

The man remained standing. He had a folded coat under one arm and no shoes. Iona looked at his feet, then caught herself and looked at his face.

“Would you let me have a look?”

“At which part?”

“The part you would like me to start with.”

He considered her. “Left heel. The right one still belongs to me.”

Iona knelt outside the opening. He passed his foot through as carefully as if he were lending her something expensive.

Behind Mara, Renn Tallow lowered the square signal board they had been carrying.

“Six people?” Renn asked.

“Including the one holding your gate,” said Senn.

Renn examined the board. A strip of paper had been pinned across its bottom edge. The paper was dry beneath the overhang, though the rest of the board bore streaks of rain.

Mara knew the form. She had used it often enough to recognize the space where a runner signed a completed delivery.

“What time did that arrive?” she asked.

“Before first light.”

“From where?”

“Inside.”

“Which office?”

Renn turned the board toward her. The strip bore two stamps and a heavy line of type: EVACUATION COMPLETE. There was no runner's signature.

“I asked that too,” Renn said.

Mara read it again, looking for a detail that would make the instruction belong to someone. The stamps were clean. The corner reserved for a name was blank.

“Then take it off the board.”

“And put what in its place?”

She almost said that it did not matter. Six people were waiting at the gate. That was enough to make the instruction wrong.

It was not enough to tell the next cart where to go.

“How many are on your road?” she asked.

Renn looked past her, toward the bend beyond the watch shelter. “Nine at the last count. More coming from the water steps.”

“Walking in?”

“They heard there was transport out.”

Senn laughed once, without amusement. “So did we.”

The wheel on the far side began to turn again.

Bram stepped into the gap beside Senn and planted his shield against the gate. The pressure took him by surprise. His boots shifted back half a pace before he found a brace against the stonework.

“You can let go,” he told Senn.

Senn did, slowly. The relief in their face disappeared when they saw Bram's shoulders settle under the weight.

“How long can you hold it?”

“Long enough to look at the wheel.”

“That wasn't the question.”

“No,” he said. “It wasn't.”

Hux crouched beside Tamsin. Through the bars, she guided his hand under the cart's brake arm.

“Up a little. Toward you. Stop.”

The arm sprang free with a crack. Hux withdrew his hand and looked at the line of rust across his palm.

“It needs a washer.”

“It needs a new brake,” Tamsin said.

“We have neither,” Senn told them.

Hux felt through his coat pockets. He found a button, a piece of wire, and a flattened metal cap. He laid the button aside.

“For the moment,” he said, “we have this.”

“Will it hold on a slope?”

“Not by itself.”

“Then I need someone at the back.”

“You have me.”

Senn nodded. Only then did they take their hands off the cart.

Vale had moved to the inner edge of the watch shelter, where chalk names crowded the wall. Some were full names. Others were descriptions. A woman in a green shawl. A boy with a dog. At shoulder height, someone had marked a date beside two short lines.

Vale took a folded sheet from their coat.

“Did you write these?” they asked Senn.

“Some.”

“May I copy them?”

Senn glanced toward Renn. “For the watch?”

“For the people looking for them.”

“Those aren't always different people.”

Vale lowered the sheet.

“All right. Tell me which ones I can take.”

Senn studied them for a moment, then pointed to three names near the bottom. “Those. Their families are waiting by the water steps. Leave the descriptions for now. I don't know who wrote them.”

Renn set the signal board against the wall.

“If they're waiting there, I can send someone.”

“You can tell them where we are going,” Senn said. “Once someone tells me.”

Iona finished wrapping the man's heel. “The old customs shed has a roof. We brought blankets. There is room for six to lie down.”

“And water?”

“Two covered jars.”

“A place to leave the cart?”

“Beside the door.”

“It comes back with me.”

Iona looked up. “You aren't coming out with them?”

“There are people at the next passage who still need it.”

“How many?”

“I haven't reached them.”

She glanced at the worn canvas cover, the improvised brake and the wheel still sitting in the drainage groove. “We could carry the people who can walk and give you a rest.”

Senn shook their head. “Help me make a trip I can repeat.”

Mara turned to Renn. “Will you keep the road open to the shed?”

“For these six.”

“For anyone who comes through.”

“If you bring fifty, that roof won't get larger.”

Mara felt the old impatience rise: the need to settle the terms, assign the work and have everyone move. Renn had always been good at finding the difficulty that came after an instruction.

Usually because someone would have to stand in it.

“Keep a count,” she said. “When the shed is full, send word. We find the next place before we bring the next cart.”

“And the people already on my road?”

“Tell them exactly what we have. Don't promise transport we haven't seen.”

Renn took the paper from the signal board. The pin caught, tearing one corner. They folded it around the pin rather than dropping either into the gutter.

“I want this back,” they said, holding it toward Mara.

“The board?”

“The answer. When you find out who sent it.”

Mara put the folded instruction in her coat. “I'll bring you what I find.”

A bell sounded beyond the gate.

Bram's shield struck the stone jamb. He bent one knee and caught the next movement before it could close the gap.

Sable had been watching the far wheel.

“It moved at the second stroke,” she said.

Tamsin looked through the bars. “Could be a release on the bell wire.”

“Or someone pulling when they hear it.”

“Can you tell?”

“Not from here.”

Mara leaned close enough to see past the cart. A watch captain stood at the wheel. His sleeve had split at the elbow. One hand worked the lever while the other remained fixed around the spoke, dragging with it when it turned.

He wore the same pattern of coat she had worn.

“Captain,” she called.

The wheel stopped.

For a moment she could hear the people beneath the canvas breathing. Then the captain lifted his head. His mouth moved, but the bell sounded over it.

The lever came down.

“Again,” Bram said through his teeth. “If you're going to talk to him, do it again.”

Mara reached for her rifle. She did not raise it yet.

“Captain. There are six people at your gate.”

The captain's free hand opened. Its fingers made a small, precise movement above the lever, as if searching for a missing handle.

Then it closed again.

Tamsin followed his hand with her eyes. “That lever has to stay up before the cart can pass.”

“Can we reach it?” Mara asked.

“From his side.”

Hux kept one hand on the freed brake arm. Iona withdrew the man's foot from the opening and asked him to keep the bandage dry. Vale tucked the three permitted names inside the folded sheet.

Senn put a hand on Bram's shoulder.

“When you can open it, say so before you move. I have to lift the wheel out of that groove.”

Bram nodded. “I'll say.”

Mara looked once at Renn. The runner had turned the board over and was marking the road to the shed on its clean back.

She passed through the gap.

Authoring transition: Hollow Constable encounter. This draft neither defeats the boss nor awards progress. The post-fight scene must pay off the lever, cart movement, six people, road capacity, consent to copy names and Mara's promise to Renn. Do not silently resolve those commitments offscreen.

Revision questions: Does the cart's physical layout remain readable? Can the dialogue be shortened where the negotiation repeats a settled point? Does Renn exert enough agency beyond responding to Mara? The first volume must later give the people on the cart an identity and a life beyond this rescue. These are editorial questions, not completed acceptance checks.
