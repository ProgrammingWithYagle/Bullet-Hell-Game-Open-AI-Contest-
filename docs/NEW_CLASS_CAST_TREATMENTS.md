# Five new disciplines: working cast treatments

The five classes are accepted additions. The people below are **working proposals** for the new playable leads, not characters already selectable or present in published scenes. Their names, designs and biographies still need revision with the actual kits and scripts. The existing seven leads keep their established identities and arcs.

All five are recent entrants to their disciplines. Earlier occupations provide knowledge, relationships and habits without making them established masters of the new class. The world has other practitioners of every discipline. Each new lead needs a main-story role, an optional personal arc, relationships beyond the party and an aftermath. An impressive skill tree cannot substitute for those things.

## Dara Flint — Swordsman — An edge worth keeping

Dara worked at a tool-grinding bench before beginning sword training. She can see an uneven edge and dislikes being watched while she fails a movement that looks simple. Her practical expertise gives her a way into conversations without making her a veteran fighter. She enjoys competitive board games and is much less composed when losing one than she would like people to know.

Her immediate concern is a damaged blade somebody expects her to use. Her larger conflict concerns the difference between learning a discipline and performing competence for an audience. An older practitioner can offer useful instruction while being wrong about the life Dara should lead. A rival can be kind in private and still want the place Dara wants.

The first chapter should give her a small, consequential failure that another person helps repair. Later pressure should involve a commitment she makes beyond fighting: maintaining tools for people who cannot reward her, or deciding whether the training arrangement still serves those who depend on it. Her personal arc should end in a choice she can sustain, not in proving that every teacher was secretly inferior.

The sword kit must earn its range, parry windows, commitment and counters. Narration can show maintenance, footwork and fatigue; Dara need not deliver a philosophy of the blade whenever she draws it. She can argue, be embarrassed and change the subject. Her class should not require a perpetually solemn personality.

## Joss Merrow — Summoner — More than an instruction

Joss used to organize deliveries for market stalls. He remembers other people's promises and has a habit of saying yes before checking whether the day contains enough hours. Summoning appeals to him partly because he wants help and partly because giving a clear instruction feels easier than asking a person for a favor. That combination can make him useful and unreliable in different ways.

His entities should become recognizable through behavior, limits and recurrence. A protective entity might repeatedly choose the same place in a formation; a small scout might find a route that is inconvenient for the person following it. These are story possibilities, not delivered abilities or a finalized creature roster. They should not all be interchangeable particles with health bars.

His personal conflict is how to remain responsible for an army's actions without pretending he can predict everything it will encounter. Another Summoner may have better technique and much worse judgment, or a modest practice that Joss initially dismisses. A living antagonist can exploit a promise without having to possess or corrupt the entire army.

Give his human relationships ordinary substance as well. Somebody should know when he is trying to sound more certain than he is. Somebody can refuse to lend him another tool until the previous one comes back. A successful arc may involve accepting a bounded commitment and doing it properly, rather than solving every need by making more entities.

The game needs explicit unit budgets, formation/focus/recall commands, ownership, damage credit and cleanup. The prose must respect those limits. No story rescue should casually summon an unlimited army when the player can command only a few formations.

## Nela Venn — Ranger — The route on the page

Nela assisted with water surveys and copied routes before taking up Ranger training. She can recognize a changed bank and still misjudge whether another person can make the same crossing she can. Her maps are careful; her explanations become too quick when she is excited. She likes routes that make sense and can take it personally when a place refuses to match her account of it.

Her first main-story task is to help establish a supply route outside the immediate gate. People already use that route for reasons her map does not record. Some may not want it made easier to find. A good shortcut for an armed party can be a bad road for a loaded cart or a family who needs to remain unnoticed.

The arc should pressure her assumptions through people with their own expertise: a boat worker, a displaced household, another Ranger who has kept a route private, and someone who profits when the official map stays wrong. The conflict should not end with discovering that Nela's first map was secretly perfect.

Archery, tracking and traps give her gameplay work distinct from sustained gunfire. They must have actual ranges, preparation and weaknesses. Her observations can be wrong, and she need not narrate every environment for the party. Give her conversations in which she cares about something other than the next trail.

## Perrin Moss — Alchemist — A usable batch

Perrin came from a dyehouse, where getting the expected color was only part of a day's work. Quantities, contaminated equipment, orders and what could be afforded mattered too. Alchemist training offers new possibilities that are exciting enough to tempt Perrin into promising a result before a mixture is properly understood.

The first useful result should solve a limited problem and leave work behind. A coating can fail in rain; a supply of ingredients can belong to people who need it for another task. A recipe's existence does not make it cheap, safe in every circumstance or available in unlimited quantity. These are fictional design constraints, not real-world chemical instructions.

Other Alchemists can disagree about proof, access and acceptable costs. One may be an antagonist who is perfectly willing to report a successful batch while assigning its failures to workers with less power. Another can be careful and unpleasant without being wrong. Perrin should have to hear criticism from someone they would rather impress.

A personal arc can follow the difference between a clever demonstration and a process other people can use reliably. It needs ordinary pleasures and relationships as well as laboratory conflict. Perrin might care deeply about a particular shade for personal reasons without that shade becoming another universal secret symbol.

The recipe/ingredient kit must be usable in solo and co-op with a reliable basic contribution. Story scarcity should create decisions while the class remains playable. Do not grant a miraculous new compound to escape a scene unless its actual mechanics and limits have been established.

## Iven Cair — Bard — When to come in

Iven worked backstage before entering Bard training. He knows how to make a room sound different and how long a performer can wait before silence feels accidental. He has much less experience being the person the room is listening to. Offstage he can be plain-spoken, observant and annoyingly interested in other people's unfinished conversations.

He is drawn to the bells because they are heard by people who did not choose the performance. That is an original question within Lorn's world, not a borrowed pathway, power sequence or plot from Lord of Mysteries. His training should distinguish music, civic signals, supernatural resonance and speculation about how they interact.

A fellow Bard can be a friend with a different purpose for performance. A teacher can want the sound preserved when Iven wants the event interrupted. A human antagonist might control venues, announcements or access to an audience without possessing supernatural control of listeners. People can enjoy a performance and still refuse the request made afterward.

His first consequential act need not be spectacular. He might notice that a familiar cue is making people move before they have heard the full instruction, then have to decide whether and how to interrupt it. The actual intervention must use implemented rules; the script cannot solve a conflict by declaring that his song changes everybody's mind.

His personal arc should include the pleasure of performing, the temptation to hide a mistake inside a confident delivery, and the work of hearing an answer he did not want. It should also give him friends who find him funny, tiresome or useful in circumstances unrelated to a stage. Quietness does not make him morally wiser than a louder character.

The proposed kit uses deliberate cadence, prepared phrases, resonance, misdirection and useful solo/cooperative effects. Input, reach, duration, interruption and stacking need clear limits. A Bard should be playable without compulsory rapid rhythm inputs, and the class must not take over every bell-related solution from the rest of the ensemble.

## Cross-cast development

These five should not arrive as five new people who all need the original seven to explain life to them. Rotate who notices, helps, makes an error and sets a limit. Shared experience can create affinity without turning every pair into agreement: Dara and Tamsin may disagree over a repair; Joss and Mara may recognize an uncomfortable habit; Nela and Vale may differ over which route should be recorded; Perrin and Hux may clash over a useful material; Iven and Sable may reach different conclusions from the same sound.

Those are potential relationships, not completed scenes. Their later scripts must respect actual established evidence, individual knowledge and the personalities that emerge in play. Side characters and antagonists remain participants with independent lives. When these leads enter production, expand the personal-arc register accordingly; do not count a name and a treatment as a completed character arc.
